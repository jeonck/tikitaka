These questions are usually acts of care disguised as questions. Someone asking whether you slept is not collecting data — they noticed something and want to check on you. Answer the *noticing*, and keep it light unless you genuinely want to go deeper.

{{< tk q="You look tired. Everything okay?" ctx="A coworker, first thing in the morning." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Just a late one —" say="Just a late one, nothing dramatic." back="I will live." why="Nothing dramatic is the phrase that closes a worry politely. I will live signals you are fine without being cold." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Bad night —" say="Bad night, honestly. I woke up at three and made the mistake of picking up my phone, and that was the end of that." back="Do you ever get stuck like that?" why="One concrete moment beats a vague I did not sleep well. The phone-at-three detail is almost universally recognized." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Is it that obvious? —" say="Is it that obvious? I was hoping the coffee was doing more work than it apparently is." back="Be honest, how bad is it?" why="Turning it into a joke about your own appearance defuses the concern and gives them an easy, warm thing to answer." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="Are you feeling better?" ctx="Your first day back after being off sick." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Much better —" say="Much better, thanks. Almost back to normal." back="How have things been here?" why="Almost is the honest hedge that stops people worrying and stops them expecting full speed. Turning to work moves the conversation on." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Getting there —" say="Getting there. The worst of it passed on the weekend, but I still sound worse than I feel." back="Did it go around the office while I was out?" why="Getting there is the standard mid-recovery answer. Separating how you sound from how you feel preempts the concerned look people give a cough." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Eighty percent —" say="About eighty percent, which I am choosing to call good enough. Any longer at home and I would have started talking to the furniture." back="Are you the rest-it-out type or the push-through type?" why="A percentage is a playful way to be precise. The last question invites a mild, safe disagreement about how people handle being ill." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="Do you work out?" ctx="Someone making conversation at a party or a gym." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Here and there —" say="Here and there. Nothing serious." back="Do you?" why="Here and there is the answer for a habit that exists but is not an identity. It avoids both bragging and apologizing." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="I walk a lot —" say="I walk a lot, which I have decided counts. I tried the gym for a few months and realized I only enjoy exercise if it takes me somewhere." back="What works for you?" why="Reframing what counts as exercise is a real and shared opinion. It also gracefully avoids claiming a routine you do not have." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="I have a membership —" say="I have a membership, which I understand is not the same thing as working out." back="Please tell me I am not alone in this." why="The gap between paying and going is one of the most reliable jokes in English. It practically guarantees a story back." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="How did you sleep?" ctx="A housemate, a partner, or a friend on a shared trip." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Like a rock —" say="Like a rock. Did not move all night." back="You?" why="Like a rock is the fixed idiom for deep sleep. It is one of those phrases that instantly sounds like a first-language speaker." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="On and off —" say="On and off. I kept half waking up and checking the time, which never once made it better." back="Was it the same for you, or is that just me?" why="Half waking up is the precise phrase for that state. Naming a pointless habit invites agreement rather than sympathy." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Ask the ceiling —" say="Ask the ceiling. It and I got to know each other pretty well between two and four." back="Any tricks that actually work for you?" why="A small absurd image lands better than complaining, and asking for tricks turns a bad night into a useful exchange." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="Are you sure you should be at work today?" ctx="You are visibly unwell and someone is gently pushing you to go home." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Probably not —" say="Probably not. I am going to finish this one thing and then go." back="" why="Conceding immediately ends the negotiation. No pass back is needed — they have got what they wanted." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="I did think about it —" say="I did think about it. There is one handover I did not want to leave hanging, but after that I am going to call it a day." back="Would you mind keeping an eye on the inbox?" why="Explaining the one reason you came in makes you sound responsible rather than stubborn, and the question converts their concern into actual help." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="You are the third —" say="You are the third person to say that, so I am starting to think there might be something in it." back="Do I really look that bad?" why="Counting the warnings is a light way to admit they are right. The closing question keeps it warm instead of defensive." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< callout type="warning" >}}
Two things to leave alone here. Do not give a full symptom report unless you are asked twice — in English, one round of detail is care, three is an imposition. And do not use **Flip** on someone who is genuinely worried about you; a joke can read as brushing them off.
{{< /callout >}}
