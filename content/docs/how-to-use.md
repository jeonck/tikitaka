Most textbooks give you *an* answer. One correct sentence, memorized, delivered — and then silence, because the other person has nothing to grab onto.

Tiki-taka is the opposite idea. In football it means short, quick passes that keep possession moving. In conversation it means the same thing: your answer is not the end of the exchange, it is a pass. It has to be catchable.

## The three routes

Every question on this site has three ways back. They are always in the same order, so you learn the shape rather than the sentence.

| Route | What it does | When to reach for it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Return** | Short, safe, instant. One clause and a question mark. | You are tired, distracted, or the person is a stranger. |
| **Add** | One concrete detail they can pick up. | You want the conversation to continue. This is the default. |
| **Flip** | Turns the question around, or plays with it. | You are comfortable, and you want warmth or humor. |

**Return** never fails. **Add** is where most real conversation lives — a detail is a handle. **Flip** is the one that makes people remember you.

## The practice loop

The answers are hidden on purpose. Reading a sentence and recognizing it is not the same skill as producing it under time pressure, and only one of those two skills helps you in a real conversation.

{{% steps %}}

### Read the question out loud

Say it as if someone just asked you. Hear the rhythm of it.

### Look only at the trigger phrase

Every route shows its opening words — *Can't complain —* or *Actually, I —*. That is the trigger. It is there because a first-language speaker does not compose a whole sentence before opening their mouth either; they start, and the rest follows.

### Say your own full answer

Out loud. Not in your head. Finish the sentence from the trigger, and end with something the other person can answer.

### Now open the card

Compare. You are not checking whether you got the *right* answer — there isn't one. You are checking whether your version gave them a handle.

### Say the card version once

Then close it and say your own again. The second attempt is the one that sticks.

{{% /steps %}}

## What is inside a card

{{< tk q="How's it going?" ctx="A colleague, passing you in the hallway." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Can't complain —" say="Can't complain. Same old, same old." back="How about you?" why="The safest answer in English. It says nothing, and that is fine — the question was not really a question." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Pretty good, actually —" say="Pretty good, actually. I finally finished that thing I have been dragging around all month." back="How's your week looking?" why="One concrete detail. They can now ask what the thing was, or complain about their own." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Ask me after —" say="Ask me after my second coffee. Right now I am running on about forty percent." back="Are you a morning person, or is that just me?" why="Self-deprecating and low-stakes. It hands them an easy opinion to have." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

The orange **pass it back** line is the important part. An answer without one is a dead ball — technically correct, conversationally finished.

{{< callout type="info" >}}
Do not try to memorize all three routes for every question. Pick one route and run it across a whole page. Next session, run a different one. The shape transfers to questions that are not on this site.
{{< /callout >}}

## A note on register

Everything here is neutral-to-casual — what you would say to a coworker, a friend of a friend, or someone at a meetup. In a job interview or with a much older stranger, lean on **Return** and **Add**, and leave **Flip** alone until you know the room.
