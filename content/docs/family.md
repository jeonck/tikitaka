---
title: Family & People
weight: 12
---

Family questions look personal but are usually structural — the asker is building a rough map of your life so the next twenty minutes have somewhere to go. You control how much of the map you hand over. One fact plus one question is almost always the right size.

{{< tk q="Do you have any siblings?" ctx="Getting to know someone over dinner." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="One older sister —" say="One older sister. We are four years apart." back="How about you?" why="A number and a gap. Four years apart is the standard way to describe sibling spacing and needs no explanation." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Two brothers —" say="Two brothers, both younger, so I got all the responsible-one training whether I wanted it or not." back="Where do you land in yours?" why="Birth order is a topic almost everyone has an opinion about. Where do you land is the natural idiom for it." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Only child —" say="Only child, which people always seem to have a theory about the moment I say it." back="Go on, what is your theory?" why="Preempting the stereotype turns a tired follow-up into a game. It works because they were probably about to make one." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="How's the family?" ctx="A colleague or neighbor who has met them once." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="All good —" say="All good, thanks. Everyone is keeping busy." back="Yours?" why="Keeping busy is the polite non-answer that signals nothing is wrong. Nobody expects more unless you offer it." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Good, mostly —" say="Good, mostly. My mother has decided to learn to swim at sixty-two, which is either inspiring or alarming depending on the day." back="Are your parents the retiring type or the new-project type?" why="One family member, one surprising detail. It gives them a person to react to instead of a category." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Loud as ever —" say="Loud as ever. We had everyone in one house last month and I have only just recovered." back="Is yours a big group or a quiet one?" why="Loud as ever is affectionate rather than critical. Big-or-quiet is a comfortable question that fits any family." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="Are you close with your parents?" ctx="A friend, a few drinks into a real conversation." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Pretty close —" say="Pretty close, yeah. We talk most weeks." back="Are you?" why="Frequency is the safest evidence of closeness. Most weeks is honest without being a commitment either way." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Closer than I was —" say="Closer than I was at twenty, definitely. It got easier once I stopped needing them to agree with me about everything." back="Did that shift for you at some point?" why="Naming a change over time is more interesting than a fixed yes. It also opens the door to something real without demanding it." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="Depends on the topic —" say="Depends entirely on the topic. Very close about food, less close about my career choices." back="Is there a subject your family has quietly agreed to avoid?" why="Splitting closeness by subject is funny because it is true. The follow-up is a genuinely good question that people enjoy answering." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="Do you have kids?" ctx="Small talk at a work event." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="Not at the moment —" say="Not at the moment, no." back="Do you?" why="Not at the moment is the neutral form. It closes the topic without inviting the follow-up that not yet sometimes attracts." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Two —" say="Two, seven and four. The seven-year-old has recently discovered that arguing is a skill you can practice." back="Are you in the middle of any of that?" why="Ages plus one current phase is exactly enough. Parents will recognize the phase; non-parents will find it funny." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="A cat —" say="No, but I have a cat who has all the same negotiating tactics and none of the school fees." back="Are you in the pets or people camp?" why="A light redirect that answers without discussing the reason. It keeps a potentially sensitive question at a comfortable distance." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< tk q="Who's this?" ctx="Someone looking at a photo on your desk or your phone." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Return" hint="That is my —" say="That is my brother, at his graduation." back="" why="Person plus occasion is a complete answer. Photos often do not need a pass back — the picture itself carries the conversation." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Add" hint="Oh, that is —" say="Oh, that is my grandmother. That photo is from her eightieth, and she insisted on doing all the cooking herself." back="Do you have anyone like that in your family?" why="One vivid, characteristic action tells them who the person is. Anyone like that is an invitation, not an interrogation." >}}
{{< tkline tone="Flip" hint="That is the reason —" say="That is the reason I know how to fix a bike, ride a horse, and lose an argument gracefully." back="Who taught you the useful things?" why="Describing someone by what they gave you is warmer than a label. The question that follows is one people genuinely like being asked." >}}
{{< /tk >}}

{{< callout type="info" >}}
If a family question lands somewhere painful, the safest exit is a short factual sentence followed by a question of your own — *It is complicated, honestly. What about yours?* Nobody in English will push past that, and you have kept the conversation alive instead of ending it.
{{< /callout >}}
